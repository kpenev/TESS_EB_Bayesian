\fancysection{Technical Approach and Methodology}
%
\label{sec:technical_approach}

To a large extent, our analysis plan is based on similar analysis from the
literature applied to \kepler{} and \tess{} LCs \citep{Reinhold_et_al_13,
Lurie_et_al_17, Windemuth_et_al_19, CantoMartins_et_al_20,
Justesen_Albrecht_21}, combining the best features of each of these analysis and
several other improvements of our own designed to increasing the reliability of
the catalog and allowing us to properly handle difficulties with some EBs
reported by these prior efforts. Our plans include extensive validation
procedures to ensure the resulting catalogs are as clean of false positives or
erroneously determined parameters as possible. Finally, parameter uncertainties
are a crucial piece of information, of almost equal importance to the values of
the parameters themselves. Our approach includes an effort to thoroughly
determine the uncertainties. Since observed properties depend on complicated
combinations of physical parameters, the uncertainty distributions are highly
non-Gaussian, and involve complicated correlations. We will rely on a Markov
chain Monte Carlo analysis to fully characterize these uncertainty
distributions, and the actual samples as well as summary statistics for each EB
in our catalog will be provided upon publication.

\subsection{Determining Eclipsing Binary Physical Parameters}
%
\label{sec:eb_parameters_technical}

\subsubsection{Lightcurve Preparation}
%
\label{sec:params_lc_preparation}

For a selected subset of stars \tess{} downloads pixel stamps with a 2-min
cadence and full images with a 30-min cadence. The stamps are processed by the
Science Processing Operations Center (SPOC) pipeline \citep{Jenkins_et_al_16}
providing  two classes of LCs: simple aperture photometry (SAP) and pre-search
data conditioning (PDC). The PDC LCs involve detrending against empirically
constructed co-trending basis vectors, which is specifically designed to
preserve astrophysical signals while removing systematics. While the PDC
corrections are not perfect, \citep{Justesen_Albrecht_21} found they are well
suited for high precision analysis of EBs. We will use the PDC LCs by default,
but for each target we also will begin by finding a maximum likelihood model
using our default modeling of PDC LCs and  a maximum likelihood model modeling
only the eclipses without the phase curve effects (see below) using only the
portions of the SAP LCs near eclipses (up to 2 times the eclipse duration)
together with a second order polynomial model for the out-of-eclipse
variability. If the parameters of the two fits differ substantially, the target
will be flagged for manual inspection and potentially re-analysis based on
appropriately prepared SAP LC (e.g. using the \texttt{Lightkurve} package
\citep{Lightkurve_18}).

For FFIs, we will use the Quick Look Pipeline (QLP) LCs \citep{Huang_et_al_20a,
Huang_et_al_20b, Kunimoto_et_al_21, Kunimoto_et_al_22}.  Those also come in two
flavors: with and without detrending. The QLP authors themselves caution that
the detrending procedure will significantly affect low frequency signals. Hence,
we begin with non-detrended LCs, and adapt the QLP detrending procedure for our
application by modifying their high-pass filter to preserve signals at or below
the orbital frequency and applying the detrending as part of the MCMC (see
\refsec{params_bayesian_analysis}) after subtracting the EB model LC. To
preserve modulations on the orbital frequency or faster, we need to modify the
minimum spacing allowed between the smoothing spline nodes used for detrending
to match the orbit. The rest of the detrending procedure of the QLP will be
preserved. It should be noted that the exact detrending used will not preclude
detecting the high signal to noise EBs we are targeting. In fact, the yield
estimates from \refsec{catalog_size} are based on the \citep{Prsa_et_al_22}
search which applied no detrending. Fig. \ref{fig:QLP_FFI_comparison} compares
the PDC stamp-based LC and undetrended QLP lightcurve from FFIs of a 2-d EB (TIC
33419790) for a single sector. The out of eclipse LC exhibits strong ellipsoidal
variatons, and to much smaller extent a mix of reflection and boosting effects.
While instrumental trends are visible in the the QLP LC, they will not
significantly distort results for the high precision sample defined in
\refsec{catalog_size}.

The first step in our analysis will be to select candidate EB LCs. For stamp
targets, \citep{Prsa_et_al_22} already conducted a thorough search, combining
multiple detection  methods, including two manual searches by citizen scientists
\citep{Eisner_et_al_21, Kristiansen_et_al_22}, a list of EB candidates selected
as \tess{} targets, and several eclipse or periodic signal search pipelines
\citep{Jenkins_et_al_16, Wheeler_Kipping_19, Kruse_et_al_19, IJspeert_et_al_21}
to identify 4584 EBs. These will constitute the list of sources we will analyze
from stamp data.

There is no equivalent catalog of EBs for FFI LCs. Instead, we will follow
\citep{Justesen_Albrecht_21} who searched the first year of \tess{} stamp data
for high signal-to-noise EBs. Their search was conducted in several steps.
First, a Lomb-Scargle periodogram was generated for each LC, and stars with no
significant power were eliminated. Selected sources were subjected to the
transit least squares (TLS) algorithm developed by \citep{Hippke_Heller_19},
again excluding targets with no significant signal. Candidates for which neither
the eclipse depths, nor the depths of every other eclipse match within
5-$\sigma$ are also discarded as false positives. For the survivors, the
detected eclipses are masked and another search is conducted to check for
secondary eclipses of eccentric binaries which are also checked for consistency
of their depths. Finally, only high signal-to-noise candidates are kept (primary
eclipse $>5$\% and secondary eclipse $>1$\%). \citep{Justesen_Albrecht_21}
report that this procedure eliminated 98\% of the input sources. However, since
we will begin with $10^7$ LCs, $\sim2\times10^5$ will pass all tests. These are
too many to examine by eye, as \citep{Justesen_Albrecht_21} did. Instead, we
will find a maximum likelihood estimate for the system parameters and
automatically reject binaries for which the residuals between the observed LC
and the model for the maximum likelihood parameters are too large.  The exact
threshold for rejection will be determined by visual inspection of several
hundred examples.  Since the likelihood function defined below is non-linear
and likely contains multiple local minima, we will use one of the built-in
global optimization algorithms of the \texttt{scipy} Python package. We will
experiment with basin hopping \citep{Wales_Doye_97}, differential evolution
\citep{Storn_Price_97}, and dual annealing \citep{Xiang_et_al_97} algorithms as
part of this project, and if no clear winner can be found we will use multiple
selecting whichever one produces the highest likelihood for each candidate. Even
after this step, we will end up with several tens of thousands of candidates.
All surviving EBs will be subjected to full analysis and included in the final
catalog, but we will manually vet the 5\,000 -- 10\,000 with tightest
constraints to produce a sub-catalog of highly reliable EBs.

\subsubsection{Lightcurve and Spectral Energy Distribution Modeling}
%
\label{sec:params_lc_sed_modeling}

The most important constraint on the physical parameters of EBs comes from
comparing predicted LCs to what \tess{} observes. We require a model for two
types of effects: each star blocking some part of the light from its companion
from reaching us (i.e. the primary and secondary eclipses) and out-of-eclipse
variability, driven by interactions between the two stars (phase curve). For the
eclipses, we will use the Python package \texttt{batman} \citep{Kreidberg_15}.
We generate two \texttt{batman} models for a given set of EB parameters,
modeling the eclipse of the primary and secondary stars separately. The two
models are then added per the corresponding brightness ratio of the stars.

Our model for the phase curve must handle ellipsoidal modulations, Doppler
beaming, and reflection effects. We will use the eccentric BEER model (eBEER)
\citep{Engel_et_al_20}, which the authors show is able to capture the phase
curve effets to better than 10\,ppm, much superior to the \tess{} precision.
Hence, the phase curve model will not introduce significant model uncertainties
compared to the observational ones. The eBEER model introduces 4 additional
parameters to the LC model, that parameterize the response of each star to
heating by its neighbour and the ratio of doppler beaming to radial velocity.
While the eBERR model begins to lose precision at extreme eccentricities since
it ignores higher order orbital harmonics, in practice this limitation is never
relevant because at short periods EBs are tidally circularized and at long
periods the phase curve effects are negligible. Binaries that are simultaneously
short period and highly eccentricy need some mechanism to excite the
eccentricty (e.g. third body), in which case many aspects of the proposed
analysis break down.

%Let $\theta$ be the angle between the line of sight direction and the line
%connecting the centers of the two stars in the binary, and $d$ be the separation
%between the two stars. Doppler beaming is proportional to the line of sight
%velocity of the primary star, and to leading order the ellipsoidal modulations
%are proportional to $\sin(2\theta)/d^3$, and the reflection effect is
%proportional to $\cos\theta/d^2$. The proportionality constants of these terms
%depend on details of the stellar atmosphere and tidal love number. In our LC
%model, we treat these constants as model parameters free to be adjusted to match
%the LC. In fact in order to accommodate imperfectly filtered instrumental
%effects or astrophysical variability not related to binary interactions, we also
%include terms proportional to $\sin\theta/d^2$ and $\cos(2\theta)/d^3$.

It should be noted that while there are much more sophisticated treatments of
these effects available, for example by the \texttt{PHOEBE} package, those
necessarily are much more computationally intensive. For example, generating a
single EB LC using \texttt{PHOEBE} with sufficient resolution for this project
takes several to several tens of seconds using a single thread on a contemporary
computer. Given that \citep{Windemuth_et_al_19} found it takes $\sim10^8$
samples ($10^6$ steps of 128 walker MCMC) for the samples to fully converge to
the desired posterior distribution, the proposed analysis is not practical for
tens of thousands of EBs even using state of the art high performance computing
facilities. In contrast, the proposed modeling is $10^3$ to $10^5$ times faster
(depending on the binary parameters) making it entirely feasible (see Facilities
and Equipment).

The apparent brightness of a system often places better constraints on the
masses than the LC alone, because mass is tightly correlated with luminosity. We
rely on parallax based distances from Gaia Data Release 2
\citep{Bailer-Jones_et_al_18}. While Gaia DR3 parallaxes are already available
\citep{GaiaDR3_22}, converting to distance and uncertainty estimates is highly
non-trivial. If DR3 distance analysis becomes available before or during this
project, we will of course us the updated distances. However, given that we are
only analyzing very bright nearby stars, the DR2 precision is more than
sufficient for our purposes, and is not the dominant source of uncertainty when
converting absolute to apparent magnitudes by a significant margin. We will use
the extinction estimate fro the \tess{} input catalog (TIC) whenever available,
using the 3D galactic dust map by \citep{Green_et_al_19} as a backup, and the
dust map by \citep{Schlegel_et_al_98} as a last resort. For LC modeling we
calculate difference it \tess{} magnitudes of the sources, and SED constraints
will be based on whichever of the following magnitudes are available:
Johnson–Cousins B and V, 2MASS J, H, K, and WISE W1 and W2.

In order to build a model, we need the radii of the primary and secondary stars,
their extinction and reddening corrected apparent magnitudes, and a limb
darkening model. Without radial velocity observations, the masses of the stars
are not constrained from photometry, because the LC depends only on orbital
period, eccentricity, and orientation, the ratio of radii, and the semimajor
axis to primary radius ratio. In order to compensate for the lack of radial
velocity data, we will instead rely on stellar evolution models to convert the
two stellar masses, and common age and metallicity for a binary to radii and
absolute magnitudes in the \tess{} bandpass for LC modeling, and other
bandpasses for SED modeling (see above). In particular, we will use isochrones
generated using the Padova and Trieste Stellar Evolutionary Code
\citep{Bressan_et_al_12, Chen_et_al_14, Chen_et_al_15, Tang_et_al_14,
Marigo_et_al_17, Pastorelli_et_al_19, Pastorelli_et_al_20}, together with
bolometric corrections and star-by-star extinction coefficients (properly
accounting for the spectral dependence of extinction) as in
\citep{Chen_et_al_19}. \citep{CMD_interface} provides on-demand isochrones for a
range of stellar properties and we have already implemented high-precision
interpolation. We will assume quadratic limb darkening, and treat the limb
darkening coefficients of each stars as model parameters to be constrained by
observations rather than using theoretical models.

In defining the likelihood function to use for Bayesian Analysis, we will follow
\citep{Windemuth_et_al_19} and allow for additional systematic uncertainties in
the LC and the SED on top of the literature values, and allow the reddening
uncertainty to be determined from the data rather than attempting to derive a
formal estimate. Altogether, our complete model has 23 parameters, listed in
Table \ref{tab:mcmc_params}. We use sum and ratio of masses, as well as
eccentricity components instead of eccentricity and longitude of periapsis in
order to reduce the correlation between the sampling parameters.

Our likelihood function ($L$) is identical to Eq. 3 of of
\citep{Windemuth_et_al_19}, but we use a more complete model for the LC and SED
to evaluate it. To within a normalization constant:
%
\begin{multline*}
%
    -2 \ln L
%
    =
%
    \sum_{i} \left(
%
        \frac{ \Delta \mathrm{LC}_i^2 }{ \sigma_{\mathrm{LC},i}^2 +
        \sigma_{\mathrm{LC}, sys}^2 }
%
        +
%
        \ln\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{LC},i}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{LC}, sys}^2\right)
%
    \right)\\
%
    +
%
    \sum_{j} \left(
%
        \frac{ \Delta \mathrm{SED}_j^2 }{ \sigma_{\mathrm{SED},j}^2 +
        \sigma_{\mathrm{SED}, sys}^2 }
%
        +
%
        \ln\left(\sigma_{\mathrm{SED},j}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{SED}, sys}^2\right)
%
    \right)
%
    +
%
    \left(\frac{\Delta E(B-V)}{\sigma_{E(B-V)}}\right)^2
%
    +
%
    \ln\sigma_{E(B-V)}^2
%
\end{multline*}
%
where $\Delta \mathrm{LC}$, $\Delta \mathrm{SED}$, and $\Delta E(B-V)$ are the
residuals of the LC, SED, and reddening respectively. Adding the $\ln\sigma^2$
terms ensures that even though uncertainties are allowed to float, the best fit
solution along with the intuitive estimates for the floating uncertainty
maximizes the likelihood. Also following \citep{Windemuth_et_al_19}, we will use
Gaussian priors on $d$ and $E(B-V)$, uniform priors on all other parameters.

As already mentioned in \refsec{params_lc_preparation}, when analyzing FFIs, we
will calculate $\Delta \mathrm{LC}$ by first subtracting the LC model for a
given sample, and then applying the QLP detrending procedure. This approach
guards against the detrending potentially modifying the EB LC modulations. Since
the detrending procedure is much less computationally intensive than building
the LC model, this will not significantly increase the required computing
resources.

MCMC sampling will be performed using the \texttt{emcee} affine invariant
ensemble sampler \citep{Foreman-Mackey_et_al_13}, and convergence will be
determined using autocorrelation times for the model parameters as well as a
procedure based on \citep{Raftery_Lewis_91} adapted specifically for
\texttt{emcee} by \citep{Patel_Penev_22} for ensuring quantiles of the
distribution have converged and are estimated to specified precision.

\subsection{Physical Parameter Validation and Determining Selection Effects}
%
\label{sec:params_validation}

A battery of validations was already performed for stamp data by
\citep{Prsa_et_al_22}, who provide our input list of targets for analysis. These
tests include:

\textit{\underline{Centroid motion:}} A test of whether the centroid of the
target shifts during eclipses, indicating the target may not be the cause of the
dimming or the presence of significant additional light.

\textit{\underline{Contamination:}} A measure of the light from nearby stars
contributing to the target lightcurve.

\textit{\underline{Out-of-transit variability:}} The significance with which the
expected phase curve effects described in \refsec{params_lc_preparation} are
detected for short period EBs (no significant signal is expected at long
periods).

\textit{\underline{Comparison between sectors:}} Separate maximum likelihood fit
will be carried out on the LCs of each \tess{} sector LC. If results differ
significantly, the target will be flagged for manual inspection.

Along with checking whether the target is a known EB, or has been flagged by the
TESS transit search pipeline. We will also adapt the three tests listed above
for FFI lightcurves.

We will also carry out four additional validation steps:

\textit{\underline{Comparison between stamp and FFI results:}} As all stamps are
also part of FFIs, an additional validation will be carried out by comparing the
parameters extracted from stamps to those for the same targets using FFI LCs.
This will serve as a test of our lightcurve preparation procedure allowing us to
fine-tune our LC dentrending procedure and highlight differences in sensitivity
to EB modulations between the two sets of LCs.

\textit{\underline{\texttt{PHOEBE}}:} We will generate a \texttt{PHOEBE} model
for each binary using the maximum likelihood parameters from MCMC to check if
the LC is consistent and whether the phase curve parameters ($c_i, s_j$ in Table
\ref{tab:mcmc_params}) are of the right order of magnitude. If not manual
inspection will be performed to determine if this is falsely selected target or
perhaps a genuine EB with rotational modulations due to a synchronously
spinning primary star. We will also use gradient descent starting from the MCMC
maximum likelihood parameters to find maximum likelihood parameters for
\texttt{PHOEBE} based modeling to check if our treatment introduces bias in the
parameters.

\textit{\underline{Comparison to \citep{Prsa_et_al_22} \tess{} EBs:}} We will
use the 4\,584 \citep{Prsa_et_al_22} EBs for the list of targets to analyze from
the \tess{} stamp data. Since they applied a very different analysis algorithm
than we propose and analyzed non-detrended lightcurves, comparing the binary
parameters will serve to validate our analysis and highlight potential pitfalls
we may need to address once confronted with the full dataset. This will also
allow us to gauge the improvement resulting from doubling or tripling the number
of observations.

\textit{\underline{Comparison to \citep{Justesen_Albrecht_21} \tess{} EBs:}} We
will compare our results against the \citep{Justesen_Albrecht_21} EBs from the
first year of \tess{}. This will be another valuable test of our approach, since
these authors built their LC model without reference to stellar evolution, and
included stellar evolution and SED modeling as a post-processing step.

\textit{\underline{Comparison to \citep{Windemuth_et_al_19} \kepler{} EBs:}}
This will be the most stringent validation of our results. We will analyze a
representative sample of \kepler{} LCs and comparing results to
\citep{Windemuth_et_al_19} to validate our algorithms. Additionally, we will
find overlapping detections allowing comparison of our results against
independent analysis applied on independent data.

These comparisons will be performed both for the stamp and the FFI EBs we will
analyze. They will allow robust determination of the reliability of the proposed
analysis and serve to quantify the completeness and the selection effects our
processing imposes on the final collection.

Since the \citep{Windemuth_et_al_19} \kepler{} EBs are based on observations
with much better time coverage and photometric precision, comparison against the
latter dataset is the best option to empirically constrain the sensitivity of
our EB detection procedure allowing the resulting catalog to be used for
statistical studies of EB populations. In order to facilitate that, we will
maintain a flag for each binary in the final catalog of whether it was selected
as a candidate by the automated selection algorithm described in
\refsec{params_lc_preparation} and also provide as a separate data product the
disposition of each LC according to the automated search (whether selected for
analysis or not), as well as flags for the subsequent rejection steps employed.

Fully characterizing the completeness of the proposed analysis is beyond the
scope of this proposal. It would require detailed injection-recovery simulations
in a carefully prepared collection of lightcurves of non-binary stars with very
similar astrophysical variability and instrumental effects as the true binary
population in the \tess{} sample (not the one we detect). Such an effort will be
on par with the work already described above and may be pursued in future
proposals if it can be justified. However, we will make every effort to
facilitate such characterization by other groups. In our data management plan
(\refsec{dmp_software}) we describe how we will ensure all our steps are easily
reproducible by independent scientists with minimal effort, allowing them to
build a proper injection-recovery pipeline if desired.

\subsection{Determining Spins}
%
\label{sec:spin_technical}

\subsubsection{Lightcurve Preparation}
%
\label{sec:spin_lc_preparation}

For detecting rotational modulation from high cadence stamp-based photometry, we
will follow many successful analyses in the literature which have all concluded
that the best approach is to use PDC detrended LCs. For FFI based LCs however,
the QLP detrending is likely to eliminate the rotational variability if the spin
period exceeds 10 days or so. As a result, we will use the raw lightcurves, but
apply a battery of validation tests to reject false signals (see
\refsec{spin_validation}). Preliminary tests show that the proposed validations
successfully reject the vast majority of false signals.

As each bright ($T<13.5$) stamp target will also have a QLP lightcurve, comparing
to the spins from PDC detrended stamp LCs to those of the same stars from FFIs
will provide a precise and accurate measurement of the false alarm rate. If that
is high in spite of the many validations, we will re-introduce the QLP
detrending but just like for the EB physical parameter analysis, modified to
preserve frequencies up to a limit. The limit will be tuned to the highest value
that achieves appropriately low false alarm rate.

Searching for periodic signals in EB LCs, indicative of rotational modulation,
needs to overcome the fact that the eclipses are likely going to be by far the
dominant periodic signal, and for short period binaries the phase curve effects
(\refsec{params_lc_sed_modeling}) will also introduce periodicities. Each EB
lightcurve will be prepared in two different ways, each of which will be
subjected to the three period search methods below to produce six versions of the
periodogram to serve as each other's cross checks (see
\refsec{spin_search_and_measurement}). The first method will simply mask out the
eclipses using the maximum likelihood model from above with appropriate padding
based on the uncertainties. For long period binaries, where phase curve effects
are negligible, this will produce lightcurves that simply combine the light of
two (approximately) isolated stars. The second method will subtract the maximum
likelihood LC model for the binary. This has the advantage of suppressing all
binary effects from the lightcurve, however the maximum likelihood parameters
will not be exactly equal to the actual physical parameters and hence small
residuals are likely to remain in the LC. We will account for this when
determining the significance to which a potential spin signal is detected by
sampling EB LC models corresponding to the MCMC samples (see
\refsec{spin_search_and_measurement}).

\subsection{Candidate Selection and Spin Period Measurements}
%
\label{sec:spin_search_and_measurement}

Finding EBs exhibiting rotational modulations and measuring their spin will rely
on three independent analyses, each applied to two differently prepared versions
of the LC (see \refsec{spin_lc_preparation}):

\textit{\underline{The autocorrelation function (ACF)}} calculates the
correlation of the lightcurve with a time shifted version of itself vs. the time
shift. We will apply the smoothing procedure used by \citep{McQuillan_et_al_13,
Lurie_et_al_17}, where the ACF is convolved with a Gaussian to smooth out high
frequency noise. The first peak of the ACF usually corresponds to the rotation
period, or in rare cases of approximately diametrically opposed (groups of)
spots half the period. In the latter case the second peak usually has higher
amplitude. Subsequent peaks of decreasing amplitude occur at integer multiples
of the spin period. The ACF only measures self-similarity in the LC. As a result
it remains reliable in the face of gradual changes in the amplitude and period
of the rotational modulation (as the star spot pattern on the stellar surface
evolves).  \citep{McQuillan_et_al_13} also argue the highest peak of the ACF is
a more robust measure of the true spin period than LSP (see below), which may
produce a higher peak at a multiple of the period if multiple spots (or groups
of spots) are present.

\textit{\underline{The Lomb-Scargle Periodogram (LSP)}} can be thought of as
least squares fit for the amplitude of a signal of a single frequency as a
function of the frequency. LSP has an advantage over ACF in that it is much
more sensitive to multiple rotation period signals (e.g. from the two stars, or
from differential rotation on one star). We will follow the procedure of
\citep{Rappaport_et_al_14, Lurie_et_al_17} for identifying multiple rotation
periods. Describing the procedure in detail in a page limited proposal is
impractical, but roughly, groups of peaks are identified after smoothing the
periodogram, and sub-peaks are identified in an appropriately defined
neighborhood of each peak group. Conditions are imposed on the peak prominence
(height relative to the adjacent minima) to select only significant peaks.

\textit{\underline{The Wavelet Transform (WT) and global wavelet spectrum
(GWS)}} is a method to estimate the power spectrum of a signal as a function of
time (see for example \citep{Grossmann_Morlet_84}), producing wavet maps giving
the spectral power vs frequency and time. Because WT is time dependent, it is
robust against the pattern of spots on the stellar surface evolving with time.
It is also a powerful techniques for correctly distinguishing the true rotation
period vs its aliases in the presence of multiple dark features on the surface
\citep{Bravo_et_al_14}. Finally, integrating the wavelet map over time gives us
another periodogram, known as the GWS, the peaks of which can be directly
compared to the ACF and LSP in an automated fashion, and like the LSP it is
sensitive to multiple periods present in the lightcurve.

We will begin the spin analysis by calculating ACF, LSP, and GWS as described
above. For the stamp EBs, it is feasible to examine all LCs manually (per
\refsec{catalog_size} we expect fewer than 2\,000 targets) and we plan to do so
in order to get a thorough understanding of the systematics, false positives,
and other features of the data and to tweak the automated procedure described
below if necessary. For FFIs however, where we expect up to few $\times10^4$
targets, we will apply automated pre-selection to reduce the number of targets
for visual inspection. The same procedure will be applied to the stamp LCs and
the results will be added to the catalog, but for those, we will manually
examine the results of the three analyses methods combined with the different LC
versions regardless of whether the target passes the automated selection.

For the smoothed ACF we will eliminate any targets showing monotonic decrease
(i.e no periodicity is detected) for periods shorter than 20d  for targets for
which \tess{} sectors do not overlap, and 30d if at least two consecutive
sectors contribute observations for that target. As a sector is observed for 27
days, it is very unlikely that periods beyond these cutoffs would be reliably
detected. We will also eliminate ACF for which the peak pattern changes
dramatically between the two methods for eliminating the binary effects
described above. For targets surviving this initial cut, we will calculate a
bootstrap false alarm probability by randomly shuffling the LC points, and for
ACF calculated from LC from which a full EB model was subtracted, also using
randomly selected samples of binary parameters and calculating the fraction of
these randomly realized LCs for which the most prominent peak exceeds the
prominence in the original ACF.

LSP has a formally defined false alarm probability for peaks of given height,
which we will use to drop low significance peaks from manual review, considering
only peaks within the period cutoffs mentioned above. We will also eliminate
peaks for which the false alarm rate differs too much between the two methods
for removing the binary signal. While not all the assumptions behind calculating
the false alarm rate are satisfied for our LCs, it is a lower limit to the real
rate so this will preserve all high significance signals. Surviving binaries
will be subjected to the bootstrap procedure already described for ACF.

WT, will be calculated only for systems passing one of the above two tests. If
the candidate period from the above is not detected in the GWS, the system will
also be discarded. Surviving systems will be flagged for manual review.

The entire automated analysis above will be performed separately for each
contiguous portion of the LC, as well as the entire LC combining all available
observations. For example all consecutive sectors during year 1 contributing
points for a target will be analyzed separately from consecutive sectors in year
3, and then all observations will be analyzed together. A target will continue
to manual review if it survives the rejection process for at least one of these.

During manual review, we will discard systems where the variability does not
appear to be caused by spot modulations (see Fig.\,1 of \citep{Lurie_et_al_17}
for example LCs showing several different kinds of periodic variability). During
the manual review, we will also manually flag systems for which the automated
period determination has erroneously selected an alias of the period. The final
catalog will include the periods detected by all analyses of all versions of the
lightcurve, including the multiple peak groups and sub-periods from LSP or WT
which have been interpreted as indicative of differential rotation or rotations
of both stars \citep{Rappaport_et_al_14, Reinhold_Gizon_15, Lurie_et_al_17}.

\subsection{Validating Measured Spins}
%
\label{sec:spin_validation}

There is already significant number of validation steps built into the procedure
for selecting targets for manual inspection described above, ensuring only
significant signals are reported and that they are robust against the LC
preparation and period measurement technique. As described above, comparing spins
from stamp-based LCs to FFI-based LCs will help determine the false alarm rate
and completeness of our FFI spin catalog, and help us fine-tune detrending if
deemed necessary. The final manual examination of the ACF and periodograms,
along side the LC itself will guard against other periodic signals being
mistakenly interpreted as rotational modulation.

In addition, our automated procedure will be validated against the
\citep{CantoMartins_et_al_20} analysis of 1\,000 TOIs which resulted in the
detection of 287 stars with unambiguous rotation. While these stars are not EBs,
comparison of the results will test all steps except the EB modulation removal
and will help us fine-tune various rejection thresholds and other parameters in
the processing before applying it to the orders of magnitude larger datasets we
will target.

We will also directly compare the rotation periods detected by us to the
\citep{Lurie_et_al_17} EB spin detections using \kepler{} LCs, which will
overlap with our detections for their brightest targets. 177 of the Lurie EB
spins are bright ($T<13.5$) and have spin periods $<13.5$\,days, most of these
should be detectable by our FFI analysis, and \citep{Prsa_et_al_22} found 86 of
those to be well detached and 48 to also have deep transits, placing them within
our expected the high precision catalog. Since the \citep{Lurie_et_al_17}
catalog is based on the much longer time coverage and much better photometric
precision \kepler{} data, the comparison will allow us to validate our
procedures, period measurements, and will allow us to estimate the completeness
of our catalog, as allowed by the limited number of overlapping targets.

We also expect to have a large number of common detections with
\citep{Oelkers_et_al_18} who used KELT observations to detect stellar rotation.
While they did not specifically target EBs, undoubtedly some of their
$6.2\times10^4$ stars with ``likely'' rotation periods will correspond to our
EBs. They specifically targeted sources from the \tess{} Input Catalog, so
matching to our targets will be unambiguous.
